**Fear of mimesis: Technofascism and intelligence in Silicon Valley** is the PhD dissertation of Anthony Burton (Simon Fraser University, 2025; D. Phil., Communication, Faculty of Communication, Art and Technology; School of Communication). Full text and metadata: [summit.sfu.ca/item/39969](https://summit.sfu.ca/item/39969). Keywords: technofascism, critical theory, critical algorithm studies, media studies, alt-right, cyberlibertarianism.

## Abstract (verbatim)

> This dissertation examines the antidemocratic thought emergent in the intersection of Silicon Valley technolibertarianism and alt-right politics in the early 21st century. Tracing the proliferation of "red pill" epistemology from its origins in The Matrix to its adoption by online misogynist communities and tech entrepreneurs, I argue that contemporary reactionary movements represent a neoliberal response to the crisis of symbolic efficiency, where traditional sources of meaning and authority have deteriorated under datafied capitalism.
>
> Through case studies of key figures including Jordan Peterson, Peter Thiel, Curtis Yarvin, and their intellectual appropriation of thinkers like René Girard and Austrian school economists, I demonstrate how computational logics—particularly axiomatization, algorithmic thinking, and "first principles" methodology—have become tools for encoding white supremacist and patriarchal worldviews as objective truths. These figures position themselves as self-creating rational actors who transcend social determination while disavowing their own sociality.
>
> Drawing on psychoanalytic theory, media studies, and critical analyses of neoliberalism, I show how Silicon Valley's "politics of no politics" and escape fantasies (seasteading, bunkers, monarchist ideologies) represent not technological innovation but reactionary attempts to preserve existing power structures through technocratic means. The dissertation reveals how the instrumentalization of critical theory by tech entrepreneurs transforms social critique into tools of domination that justify elimination of democratic processes in favor of sovereign control.
>
> Ultimately, this work contributes to understanding how digital technologies and their associated epistemologies are deeply embedded within colonial, patriarchal, and capitalist logics that shape contemporary forms of authoritarianism. By examining the convergence of computational thinking and reactionary politics, I demonstrate how appeals to rationality and intelligence function as masks for the reproduction of white male supremacy in the digital age, offering crucial insights for scholars of media, technology, gender, and political theory.

1. **Introduction** — the red pill; disorder and the crisis of symbolic efficiency; misogyny and rationality; male fantasies; cyberlibertarianism; the "technological bros."
2. **Dr. Jordan Peterson and the autogenetic sovereign** — Peterson as computationalist expertise; the Darwinian myth; regeneration and gender.
3. **Experts, intellectuals, and a dark web of sovereigns** — the Intellectual Dark Web as a sovereignty machine; the Žižek–Peterson 2019 debate reexamined.
4. **First principles, mimesis, and Silicon Valley ideology** (p. 79) — first principles and Silicon Valley ideology; fear of flows; the prime mover and the sovereign; the thinking monarch; white principles; mimesis and infringement on the sovereign.
5. **René Girard's neoliberal politics of envy** (p. 108) — desire for bros; scapegoating and paranoia; mimetic media and the mirror self; execution, action, and technologization.
6. **Excursus: Praxeological theory** (p. 154) — the Austrian School; reactionary knowledgemaking; praxeology as philosophy; libertarian axiomatics; heterodoxy and victimhood.
7. **Epilogue** (p. 169).
